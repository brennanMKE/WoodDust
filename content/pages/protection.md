# Protecting yourself from wood dust

All wood dust is hazardous ([why](/risks)), but most of the risk can be controlled with a few habits. The order you tackle them in matters.

## Start at the top: the hierarchy of controls

Safety agencies rank protective measures from most to least effective. This is the [hierarchy of controls](/glossary#hierarchy-of-controls).[^NIOSH-HierarchyOfControls]

1. **Remove or reduce the dust at its source.** Choose a less dusty process where you can, such as a sharp hand plane or scraper instead of sanding, which makes the finest dust.[^HSE-EH40]
2. **Capture it at the tool.** Extraction at the point where dust is made is the main control. It protects everyone in the room, not just you.[^HSE-WIS23]
3. **Change how you work.** Cut down how long and how often you do dusty jobs, keep others away, and clean up safely.[^NIOSH-HierarchyOfControls]
4. **Wear protection.** A respirator, eye protection and suitable clothing come last because they only protect the wearer and only work when worn correctly.[^HSE-WIS23][^NIOSH-HierarchyOfControls]

Most shops need all four. HSE expects that sawing, routing and sanding will usually still need a respirator even with good extraction.[^HSE-WIS23]

## Why the order matters

- Engineering controls such as extraction work without relying on people remembering to do something.[^NIOSH-HierarchyOfControls]
- Respirators fail quietly: a poor fit, a beard or a clogged filter lets dust in without you noticing.[^HSE-WIS23]
- For dusts that cause asthma or cancer, the goal is not just to stay under a limit. It is to keep exposure as low as is reasonably practicable.[^HSE-EH40]

## The checklist

Use this as a quick check before a dusty job. A [printable version](/print/shop-poster) is available.

**At the source**
- [ ] Every machine that makes dust is connected to extraction, and the extraction is on.
- [ ] Sanders are connected to an extractor or vacuum. On-tool extraction can cut sander dust emissions a lot; one NIOSH design cut them by about 90%.[^NIOSH-HC8]
- [ ] Blast gates and hoods are set for the tool you're using.[^HSE-WIS23]

**In the air**
- [ ] Filters on collectors and vacuums are clean and in good condition.[^HSE-WIS23]
- [ ] Fine dust is checked from time to time, for example with a bright light shone across the work area.[^HSE-WIS23]

**On you**
- [ ] A respirator rated for fine dust, fitted to your face, not a nuisance dust mask.[^HSE-WIS23]
- [ ] Clean-shaven where the mask seals, or a loose-fitting powered respirator.[^HSE-WoodDust][^NIOSH-PAPR]
- [ ] Safety glasses or goggles.[^NIOSH-EyeSafety]
- [ ] Long sleeves or a shop coat that stays in the shop.[^NIOSH-TakeHome]

**Afterwards**
- [ ] Clean with a dust-rated vacuum, never a broom or compressed air.[^HSE-WoodDust]
- [ ] Change out of dusty clothes and wash your hands before going indoors.[^NIOSH-TakeHome]
- [ ] Rags used with oil finishes are dealt with safely ([fire risks](/protection/fire-and-explosion)).

## Hobby shops and workplaces

Workplace rules apply to employers, but the hazards are the same in a garage. If you use a respirator voluntarily at home, the same basics apply: use a certified respirator, follow the maker's instructions, and use it only against the hazard it is designed for.[^OSHA-1910-134-AppD] In UK workplaces, employers are expected to control wood dust with extraction, respirators and health checks.[^HSE-COSHH-Woodworking]

## If you notice symptoms

A runny nose, cough or wheeze that eases on days away from the shop can be an early sign of a wood dust allergy.[^HSE-INDG172] See [Symptoms](/symptoms) and talk to a doctor.
