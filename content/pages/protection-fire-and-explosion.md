# Fire and explosion risks from wood dust

Wood dust is fuel. Fine dust burns fast, and a cloud of it in an enclosed space can explode.[^HSE-WIS32] OSHA lists wood among the materials whose dust can explode.[^OSHA-CombustibleDust] In most woodshops the realistic risks are fires that start in dust collectors and fires from finishing rags, so that is where to focus.

## How dust becomes a fire or explosion

- A cloud of fine dust burning in the open makes a flash fire. Inside a collector, duct or room, pressure can build into an explosion.[^HSE-WIS32]
- HSE advises treating all wood waste as potentially explosive unless tested. Dust with a mean particle size under 200 microns usually carries an explosion risk.[^HSE-WIS32]
- Sanding makes the finest, most explosive dust. Hardwood machining and board materials such as MDF and chipboard also make a lot of fine dust.[^HSE-WIS32]
- An explosive cloud is very thick. For wood flour, the lowest explosive concentration is about 60 g/m³, which looks like dense fog.[^HSE-WoodFire] That is 60,000 times the NIOSH recommended health limit of 1 mg/m³.[^NIOSH-REL] Clouds that thick are most likely inside collectors and ducts, or when settled dust is thrown into the air by a first blast.[^HSE-WIS32]
- **Secondary explosions.** A first blast inside a collector can shake loose dust settled on beams and ledges and ignite it. The second explosion is usually worse than the first.[^HSE-WIS32] This is why good housekeeping matters for fire as well as health.

Building explosions are rare outside industries such as chipboard making, but fires are not: every year premises are badly damaged by wood dust fires, usually starting in extraction equipment.[^HSE-WIS23]

## Dust collector fires

Many fires start inside the extraction system.[^HSE-WoodFire]

- **Ignition sources** include open flames, faulty or unsuitable electrics, and sparks from impacts.[^HSE-WIS32]
- **Metal in the system.** Nails, screws or metal scraps sucked into a collector, or metal in offcuts being sanded, can make friction sparks. These can set sawdust smoldering, and the airflow can fan it into a fire. Keep metal out of the system.[^HSE-WIS32]
- **Static.** Use conductive ductwork connected to earth (ground), and earth the metal helix of flexible hose.[^HSE-WIS32][^HSE-WIS23]
- **Hot work.** Welding and cutting near dust has caused many incidents. Clean the area and isolate the system first.[^HSE-WIS32]
- **Where it sits.** Collectors are best sited outside, away from people. An open fabric-bag collector indoors should be kept well away (HSE suggests at least 3 m) from workers, walkways and anything that can burn, or enclosed.[^HSE-WIS32]

### Good habits with a collector

- Run the fan for a while after the machines stop so the ducts clear.[^HSE-WIS32]
- Keep the system dust-tight and replace worn seals and gaskets.[^HSE-WIS32]
- Empty bags and bins regularly.[^HSE-WIS32]
- If you smell smoldering, switch off the airflow before you investigate. Smoldering fires often come before explosions.[^HSE-WIS32]
- Make sure you can turn the fan off from a safe place.[^HSE-WIS32]
- On a dust fire, a gentle water spray is better than a jet, which can throw burning dust into the air.[^HSE-WIS32] For anything beyond a very small fire, get out and call the fire service.

## Oily rags from finishing

Rags used with oil-based finishes such as oil paints, varnishes and stains give off heat as the oil dries. Piled up or stuffed in a box or bin, they can smolder and catch fire on their own.[^CambridgeFD-2023]

- Never leave used rags in a pile, a bag or the shop trash.
- Lay them flat to dry outdoors, away from buildings, or put them in a metal container with a tight lid, covered in water.
- Follow the finish label and your local fire service or waste rules for disposal.

## Housekeeping is fire prevention

- Don't let dust build up on beams, shelves, lights or the tops of machines. HSE recommends regular cleaning with HEPA-type vacuums.[^HSE-WIS32]
- Keep the area around heaters, motors and electrical panels clear of dust and shavings.
- Store finishes and solvents closed and away from ignition sources.

See [Cleanup](/protection/cleanup) for safe cleaning methods.

## In a workplace

OSHA covers combustible dust through general standards and an inspection emphasis program rather than a wood-dust-specific rule.[^OSHA-WoodDust] In the UK, HSE's guidance on safe wood waste collection covers collector design, explosion relief and siting.[^HSE-WIS32] Larger shops should get advice from a competent engineer and their fire authority.

## Checklist

- [ ] Collector outside, or well away from people and combustibles.
- [ ] Metal ducts, grounded flexible hose.
- [ ] Nothing metal goes into the collector.
- [ ] Fan runs on briefly after machines stop.
- [ ] Dust not allowed to build up on ledges and beams.
- [ ] Oily rags dried flat outdoors or sealed in a metal can with water.
- [ ] Extinguisher within reach; know how to shut the collector off.
