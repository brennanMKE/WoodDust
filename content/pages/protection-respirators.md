# Respirators for wood dust

A respirator is your last line of defense. It protects only the person wearing it, and only when it fits and is worn the whole time.[^HSE-WIS23] Use it alongside [dust collection](/protection/dust-collection), not instead of it.[^NIOSH-HierarchyOfControls] Even with good extraction, sawing, routing and sanding usually still call for one.[^HSE-WIS23]

## Dust masks are not respirators

Thin "nuisance dust" masks do not filter out wood dust and give no real protection.[^HSE-WIS23] Look instead for a respirator certified for particles: in the US, a NIOSH approval with a filter class such as N95 or P100;[^NIOSH-FFR] in the UK and EU, a CE or UKCA marking.[^HSE-WIS23]

## The main types

**Disposable filtering facepiece** (N95, P100, FFP3). The whole mask is the filter.[^NIOSH-FFR] Cheap and light, but breathing through it takes more effort, so HSE says it is less suited to hard work or wearing for more than about an hour.[^HSE-WIS23]

**Reusable half or full facepiece** (elastomeric). A rubber or silicone mask with replaceable filters or cartridges. It can be cleaned and reused, and a full facepiece also covers the eyes.[^NIOSH-Elastomeric]

**Powered air-purifying respirator** (PAPR). A battery blower pushes filtered air into a mask, hood or helmet. Breathing is easy, and hood or helmet styles also protect the eyes.[^NIOSH-PAPR]

## What the filter letters and numbers mean

US particle filters have a letter and a number.[^NIOSH-FFR]

- **N, R or P** is resistance to oil mist: N is not oil resistant, R is somewhat resistant, P is strongly resistant.
- **95, 99 or 100** is the minimum share of particles caught in testing: 95%, 99% or 99.97%.

N-series filters are meant for particles without oil, which covers plain wood dust, so for sawing and sanding N95 and P100 differ mainly in filter efficiency.[^NIOSH-FFR]

The filter is only part of the story. The type of facepiece, and how well it seals, sets how much protection you can expect. That is why agencies give each type an [assigned protection factor](/glossary#assigned-protection-factor):

- OSHA assigns 10 to half masks, including N95-style disposables; 50 to full facepieces; and 25 to 1,000 to PAPRs, depending on the headpiece.[^OSHA-1910-134]
- For wood dust, HSE asks for a protection factor of at least 20, which for disposables usually means FFP3.[^HSE-WIS23]

If you sand for long stretches or work in heavy dust, a reusable mask or a PAPR is the better choice.[^HSE-WIS23] A full facepiece or PAPR also carries a higher protection factor.[^OSHA-1910-134]

## Fit is everything

- **Fit testing.** Faces vary, so a tight-fitting respirator needs a fit test to confirm it seals on you.[^HSE-WIS23] Tests are either qualitative (can you taste or smell a test agent?) or quantitative (an instrument measures leakage).[^NIOSH-FitTesting] You may need to try more than one model or size.[^HSE-WIS23]
- **Repeat it.** US workplace rules call for a fit test before first use, each year, and whenever you change model or size.[^OSHA-1910-134] Retest after a big weight change or dental or facial changes.[^NIOSH-FitTesting]
- **Seal check every time.** Each time you put it on, breathe in sharply (the mask should pull in slightly) and breathe out (slight pressure, no leaks), following the maker's method. A seal check does not replace a fit test.[^NIOSH-FFR]
- **Beards and stubble break the seal.** Facial hair where the mask touches skin lets dusty air leak in.[^HSE-FitTesting] Glasses can lift a mask too.[^HSE-WIS23] If you keep a beard, use a loose-fitting PAPR hood or helmet, which needs no seal on the face.[^NIOSH-PAPR][^NIOSH-FitTesting]

Your respirator supplier or maker can usually help you find a fit tester.[^HSE-WIS23] HSE publishes guidance on what a fit test should cover.[^HSE-INDG479]

## When to replace filters

- **Disposable masks:** HSE advises discarding them after each shift, or sooner if damaged, dirty, harder to breathe through, or past their expiry date.[^HSE-WIS23] NIOSH also says to replace them when damaged, soiled or harder to breathe through.[^NIOSH-FFR]
- **Reusable masks:** follow the maker's instructions for filter changes and cleaning.[^HSE-WIS23] Store the mask clean and away from dust.[^HSE-WIS23]
- **PAPRs:** keep batteries charged and filters within their service life, per the maker.[^NIOSH-PAPR]

## Finishing needs different cartridges

Dust filters do not stop gases or vapors from solvents, paints and many finishes.[^NIOSH-FFR][^OSHA-1910-134-AppD] For spraying or wiping solvent-based finishes, use a reusable respirator with cartridges certified for the vapors listed on the product label, often combined with a particle filter.[^NIOSH-Elastomeric] Vapor cartridges have a limited service life, so unless they have an end-of-service-life indicator they need to be changed on a set schedule.[^OSHA-1910-134] Respirators that filter air are not suitable where oxygen may be low, such as a confined space.[^HSE-WIS23]

## In a workplace

US employers who require respirators must run a written program that includes medical evaluation, fit testing and training.[^NIOSH-Respirators][^OSHA-1910-134] UK guidance on choosing respirators is in HSE's HSG53.[^HSE-HSG53] If you choose to wear one where it isn't required, use a certified respirator and follow the maker's instructions.[^OSHA-1910-134-AppD]

## Checklist

- [ ] Certified particle respirator, not a nuisance dust mask.
- [ ] Fit tested on your face; seal check each time you put it on.
- [ ] Clean-shaven at the seal, or a loose-fitting PAPR.
- [ ] Worn for the whole dusty job, including cleanup and emptying the collector.
- [ ] Filters replaced on schedule or when breathing gets harder.
- [ ] Vapor cartridges for solvent-based finishes.
