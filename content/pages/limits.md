# Wood dust exposure limits

Exposure limits set the maximum average amount of dust in the air a worker may breathe over a shift. Different agencies set different numbers, and some are legal requirements while others are recommendations. None of them marks a line between safe and unsafe.

## The numbers side by side

All values are in milligrams of dust per cubic meter of air (mg/m³), averaged over a full shift unless noted.

| Body | Hardwood | Softwood | Western red cedar | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OSHA PEL (US federal)[^OSHA-CSI] | 15 total / 5 respirable | 15 total / 5 respirable | 15 total / 5 respirable | Legal limit; general "particulates not otherwise regulated" limit, not specific to wood |
| OSHA 1989 limit (vacated)[^NIOSH-NPG-AppG] | 5 (STEL 10) | 5 (STEL 10) | 2.5 | Not enforceable |
| Cal/OSHA PEL (California)[^OSHA-CSI] | 2 (STEL 5) | 2 (STEL 5) | 0.5 | Legal limit in California |
| NIOSH REL[^NIOSH-REL] | 1 | 1 | 1 | Recommendation |
| ACGIH TLV (inhalable)[^OSHA-CSI] | 1 | 1 | 0.5 | Recommendation |
| EU binding limit (inhalable)[^EU-CMRD] | 2 | none (mixtures with hardwood: 2) | none specific | Legal limit in EU member states |
| UK WEL, EH40[^HSE-EH40] | 3 (inhalable) | 5 | 5 (softwood; no specific entry) | Legal limit in Great Britain |

STEL is a 15-minute [short-term exposure limit](/glossary#short-term-exposure-limit).

## Notes on each

**OSHA (US federal).** There is no wood-dust-specific federal limit. Wood dust falls under OSHA's limits for particulates not otherwise regulated: 15 mg/m³ total dust and 5 mg/m³ respirable dust, as 8-hour averages.[^OSHA-CSI][^OSHA-AnnotatedZ1]

**OSHA 1989 (history).** In 1989 OSHA set wood dust limits of 5 mg/m³ (with a 10 mg/m³ short-term limit) and 2.5 mg/m³ for western red cedar.[^NIOSH-PEL88-WoodDust] A 1992 court decision vacated them, so they no longer apply.[^NIOSH-NPG-AppG][^OSHA-Interp-1993-04-22]

**Cal/OSHA.** California sets 2 mg/m³ for soft and hardwoods (short-term limit 5 mg/m³) and 0.5 mg/m³ for western red cedar.[^OSHA-CSI]

**NIOSH.** NIOSH recommends 1 mg/m³, averaged over up to a 10-hour day in a 40-hour week, and treats wood dust as a potential occupational carcinogen.[^NIOSH-REL]

**ACGIH.** ACGIH recommends 1 mg/m³ inhalable dust for all species except western red cedar, which is 0.5 mg/m³.[^OSHA-CSI] It also classes oak and beech as confirmed human carcinogens (A1); birch, mahogany, teak and walnut as suspected human carcinogens (A2); and all other wood dusts, including western red cedar, in class A4.[^OSHA-CSI] It flags western red cedar as both a skin and a respiratory sensitizer.[^OSHA-CSI]

**European Union.** The binding limit for hardwood dust is 2 mg/m³ inhalable, in force since 17 January 2023 (3 mg/m³ before that). When hardwood dust is mixed with other wood dust, the limit applies to all of it.[^EU-CMRD][^EU-2017-2398] There is no EU limit for softwood dust alone.[^EU-CMRD]

**United Kingdom.** EH40 sets 3 mg/m³ for hardwood dust (inhalable) and 5 mg/m³ for softwood dust. If hardwood is mixed with softwood, the hardwood limit applies to all of the dust.[^HSE-EH40]

## What the terms mean

- **mg/m³** is the weight of dust, in milligrams, in one cubic meter of air (a cube one meter on each side). A milligram is a thousandth of a gram.
- **[Time-weighted average](/glossary#time-weighted-average)** is the average concentration over a work period, usually 8 hours. Because an average can hide short peaks, some limits also come with a 15-minute short-term limit.[^HSE-EH40]
- **[Inhalable dust](/glossary#inhalable-dust)** is roughly the dust that gets into your nose and mouth when you breathe. **[Respirable dust](/glossary#respirable-dust)** is the finer part that reaches the deepest parts of the lungs.[^HSE-EH40] Limits for different fractions are not directly comparable.
- **Legal vs recommended.** OSHA, Cal/OSHA, EU and UK limits are law for employers in those places.[^OSHA-CSI][^EU-CMRD][^HSE-EH40] NIOSH and ACGIH values are recommendations.[^NIOSH-REL][^OSHA-CSI]

## Limits are not safety lines

- Wood dust is classed as a human carcinogen.[^IARC-62] For substances that cause cancer or asthma, UK law requires exposure to be kept as low as reasonably practicable, even below the limit.[^HSE-EH40]
- Research reviews report health effects linked to exposure levels below some of the legal limits above, and more symptoms with higher cumulative exposure.[^Baatjies-2023]
- Once someone is sensitized, tiny amounts can trigger symptoms.[^HSE-EH40]

Use the lowest limits (around 1 mg/m³) as a rough guide, and aim lower.

## What 1 mg/m³ looks like in a hobby shop

Picture a one-car garage shop about 6 m long, 3 m wide and 2.5 m high. That's 45 m³ of air. At 1 mg/m³, the dust floating in the whole room weighs 45 mg, a small fraction of a gram, spread so thin you can't see it. Airborne fine dust is normally invisible without special lighting.[^HSE-WIS23]

So:

- **You can't judge dust levels by eye.** A shop that looks clean can be over the limit, and a dusty-looking floor tells you little about the air.
- **The dustiest jobs** are sawing, routing, turning, sanding, emptying collector bags, and dry sweeping or blowing dust with compressed air.[^HSE-WIS23] Those are the ones to control first.
- **Measuring** exposure against these limits means drawing air through a filter with a sampling pump and weighing the dust collected.[^HSE-EH40] For most hobby shops, it's more practical to assume the dust is significant and control it: see [Protection](/protection).
